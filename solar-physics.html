Research Field 06

Solar Physics

Discover how our star produces energy, generates magnetic activity, and influences the space environment around Earth and throughout the Solar System.

What Is Solar Physics?

Solar physics is the study of the Sun's structure, magnetic fields, atmosphere, and changing activity. The Sun is a star made mostly of hydrogen and helium. Its energy is produced by nuclear fusion in its core, where hydrogen nuclei combine to form helium.

Understanding the Sun helps scientists explain how stars work, predict some forms of space weather, and protect satellites, astronauts, communication systems, and electrical infrastructure.

Layers of the Sun

Core

The central region where nuclear fusion releases the energy that eventually travels outward through the Sun.

Radiative Zone

Energy moves outward mainly through radiation, repeatedly interacting with matter along the way.

Convective Zone

Hot plasma rises and cooler plasma sinks, carrying energy toward the Sun's visible surface.

Photosphere

The visible layer from which most of the sunlight we see directly escapes into space.

Chromosphere

A layer above the photosphere that can be observed especially well using specific wavelengths of light.

Corona

The Sun's extremely hot outer atmosphere, extending millions of kilometres into space and feeding the solar wind.

Solar Activity

Sunspots

Darker, cooler regions on the photosphere associated with concentrated magnetic fields. Their numbers change over time.

Solar Flares

Sudden releases of magnetic energy that produce intense radiation across the electromagnetic spectrum.

Coronal Mass Ejections

Large eruptions of magnetised plasma from the Sun's corona. Earth-directed events can disturb Earth's magnetic environment.

Solar Prominences

Large structures of relatively cool, dense plasma held above the Sun's surface by magnetic fields.

Solar Wind

A continuous flow of charged particles escaping from the Sun and travelling through interplanetary space.

Magnetic Reconnection

A process in which magnetic field configurations change, releasing energy and helping drive solar eruptions.

The Solar Activity Cycle

The Sun's magnetic activity follows an approximately 11-year cycle. The number of sunspots and the frequency of many solar eruptions generally rise toward solar maximum and decline toward solar minimum. The cycle is not perfectly regular.

During this cycle, the Sun's large-scale magnetic field also changes and reverses polarity around solar maximum. Scientists use sunspot records, spacecraft, and ground-based observatories to track these changes.

Remember: A solar flare and a coronal mass ejection are different phenomena. A flare is a burst of radiation; a CME is a large expulsion of plasma and magnetic field. They can occur together, but one does not always accompany the other.

What Is Space Weather?

Space weather describes changing conditions in space caused mainly by solar activity. When energetic particles or solar eruptions interact with Earth's magnetic field and atmosphere, they can produce both beautiful effects and technological challenges.

Auroras

Charged particles interacting with gases in Earth's upper atmosphere produce auroras near the polar regions and, during strong geomagnetic storms, sometimes at lower latitudes.

Satellites

Solar activity can increase radiation exposure, interfere with electronics, and affect satellite operations.

Communications

Solar radiation and geomagnetic disturbances can disrupt some radio communications and navigation signals.

Power Grids

Strong geomagnetic storms can induce currents in long conductors and create challenges for electrical power systems.

Astronaut Safety

Solar energetic particle events can increase radiation risks for astronauts beyond Earth's protective environment.

Spacecraft Planning

Solar observations help mission teams assess radiation hazards and plan for changing space conditions.

How Do Scientists Study the Sun?

  • Solar telescopes: Observe the Sun in visible light and other wavelengths.
  • Helioseismology: Uses waves travelling through the Sun to investigate its interior.
  • Magnetograms: Map magnetic field strength and direction across the solar surface.
  • Spacecraft: Observe solar radiation, particles, the corona, and the solar wind from space.
  • Computer models: Simulate solar magnetic fields, plasma behaviour, and eruptive events.

Important Solar Missions

  • Solar Dynamics Observatory (SDO): Monitors the Sun and its changing activity.
  • Solar and Heliospheric Observatory (SOHO): Studies the Sun's interior, atmosphere, and solar wind.
  • Parker Solar Probe: Travels through the Sun's outer atmosphere to investigate its heating and solar wind.
  • Solar Orbiter: Studies the Sun and its polar regions using a combination of remote sensing and in-situ measurements.

Questions Scientists Are Investigating

  • Why is the Sun's corona much hotter than its visible surface?
  • How are solar flares and coronal mass ejections triggered?
  • How does the solar wind accelerate away from the Sun?
  • How accurately can scientists forecast severe space weather?
  • How will the Sun's activity change over future solar cycles?
