Mercury

The smallest planet.

Mercury is the closest planet to the Sun and the smallest of the eight planets. Its ancient, cratered surface preserves evidence of billions of years of impacts. Despite its proximity to the Sun, Mercury is not the hottest planet—that distinction belongs to Venus, whose dense atmosphere traps heat.

PLANET PROFILE

Understanding Mercury

Overview

Mercury is a terrestrial planet with a large metallic core relative to its overall size. Its surface resembles the Moon in many ways, with impact craters, ancient plains and long cliffs formed as the planet gradually cooled and contracted.

Because Mercury has almost no substantial atmosphere, it cannot retain heat effectively. Daytime temperatures can reach about 430°C, while nighttime temperatures can fall to around −180°C. Its slow rotation and long solar day contribute to its unusual thermal environment.

Scientists study Mercury to understand how rocky planets form, how their interiors evolve, and how intense solar radiation affects worlds close to their stars.

SCIENCE & GEOLOGY

A planet of extremes

Surface and geology

Mercury's surface is covered with craters formed by collisions with asteroids and comets. The Caloris Basin is one of its largest known impact structures. Long, steep scarps cross parts of the planet, evidence that Mercury contracted as its interior cooled.

Interior and magnetic field

Mercury has an unusually large iron-rich core. Measurements indicate that it possesses a global magnetic field, much weaker than Earth's. Studying the field helps researchers investigate the planet's interior and its partially liquid outer core.

Exosphere and temperature

Mercury's exosphere is extremely sparse and is not like Earth's atmosphere. Atoms are supplied and removed by processes including solar radiation, micrometeoroid impacts and interactions with the surface. With little insulation, the surface experiences extreme temperature changes.

Water ice and polar craters

Some permanently shadowed craters near Mercury's poles contain evidence of water ice. Although the planet is close to the Sun, crater floors that never receive direct sunlight can remain extremely cold. This makes Mercury an important world for studying volatile materials.

EXPLORATION HISTORY

Missions to Mercury

NASA · 1974–1975

Mariner 10

Mariner 10 was the first spacecraft to visit Mercury. During three flybys, it photographed portions of the planet's surface and gathered important data about its magnetic field and extremely thin exosphere. Its observations transformed Mercury from a poorly known world into a planet that scientists could study in detail.

Mission status: Completed
NASA · 2004–2015

MESSENGER

MESSENGER became the first spacecraft to orbit Mercury in 2011. It mapped the planet extensively, measured its magnetic field and chemical composition, and provided evidence of water ice in permanently shadowed polar regions. The mission ended in 2015 when the spacecraft impacted Mercury.

Mission status: Completed
ESA / JAXA

BepiColombo

BepiColombo is a joint mission of the European Space Agency and the Japan Aerospace Exploration Agency. Its spacecraft are designed to investigate Mercury's interior, surface, exosphere and magnetosphere. The mission uses a complex series of planetary flybys before entering its planned operational orbits.

Mission status: En route; check official updates
WHY IT MATTERS

What missions reveal

Spacecraft measurements help scientists test theories about Mercury's unusually large core, magnetic field, geological history and polar ice. Combining images, spectroscopy and gravity measurements allows researchers to investigate how Mercury formed and how rocky planets change over time.

Research field: Planetary science
OPEN QUESTIONS

What are scientists investigating?

Mercury still presents major scientific questions. Why is its metallic core so large compared with the planet's total size? How did its magnetic field evolve? What is the composition of its permanently shadowed polar deposits, and how much water ice do they contain? How have volcanic activity, impacts and contraction shaped the surface through geological time?

Data from earlier missions and the BepiColombo mission are helping scientists investigate these questions. New measurements can refine existing models and reveal details that earlier spacecraft could not observe.
