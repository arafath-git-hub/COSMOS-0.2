URANUS

The Tilted Ice Giant

Pale cyan clouds, faint rings and an extraordinary sideways tilt make Uranus one of the Solar System's most unusual planets. This distant ice giant experiences extreme seasons and holds important clues about how planetary systems form and evolve.

A world of extremes

Inside the tilted giant

Uranus is different from the gas giants Jupiter and Saturn. Its interior, atmospheric chemistry and unusual orientation make it a major target for future planetary exploration.

01 / ROTATION

A planet on its side

Uranus has an axial tilt of about 98 degrees. Scientists suspect a major collision early in its history may have contributed to this unusual orientation.

02 / ATMOSPHERE

A pale cyan atmosphere

Hydrogen and helium dominate Uranus's atmosphere, with methane absorbing red wavelengths of sunlight and helping produce its blue-green appearance.

03 / SEASONS

Extreme seasonal cycles

Uranus takes 84 Earth years to orbit the Sun. Its extreme tilt causes long seasons, with each pole experiencing extended periods of sunlight and darkness.

04 / INTERIOR

A deep, icy interior

Uranus is thought to contain a dense mixture of water, ammonia and methane beneath its atmosphere. The extreme pressure inside changes how these materials behave.

05 / RINGS

Dark and narrow rings

Uranus has 13 known rings. They are generally narrow and dark, making them difficult to observe from Earth without powerful telescopes or spacecraft.

06 / MAGNETISM

An unusual magnetic field

Uranus's magnetic field is strongly tilted relative to its rotation axis and offset from the planet's center, creating a complex magnetosphere.

Planetary mystery

Why does Uranus rotate sideways?

Most planets rotate with their axes relatively upright compared with their orbital planes. Uranus is the exception: its axis is tilted so dramatically that it essentially rolls around the Sun.

A giant impact is a leading explanation, but the planet's formation and later evolution remain active research topics. A dedicated orbiter could help scientists test these ideas.

Natural satellites

Meet Uranus's moons

Uranus has a diverse moon system. Several major moons show signs of complex geological histories, while smaller satellites occupy the region around its rings.

Ariel

A relatively bright moon with valleys, fractures and evidence of past geological activity on its icy surface.

Titania

Uranus's largest moon, featuring large canyons and impact craters that preserve clues to its geological past.

Oberon

An outer major moon with a heavily cratered surface and dark deposits, likely recording a long history of impacts.

Miranda

A small moon with dramatic cliffs, grooves and unusual terrain that has led to several competing geological theories.

Humanity's exploration

Missions to Uranus

Voyager 2 remains the only spacecraft to have visited Uranus. Earth-based observatories and space telescopes continue to study the planet while scientists consider future missions.

1986

Voyager 2

Made the only close flyby of Uranus, revealing details about its atmosphere, magnetic field, rings and moons.

Frontiers of science

What scientists still want to know

A closer look at Uranus could transform our understanding of ice giants and help explain the many similar planets discovered around other stars.

01

What tilted the planet?

Scientists are investigating whether one or more early collisions can explain Uranus's extreme axial tilt.

02

How does its interior work?

Measurements of gravity and magnetic fields could reveal how mass and materials are distributed deep inside the planet.

03

What drives its atmosphere?

Researchers want to understand how sunlight, internal heat and seasonal cycles shape clouds and atmospheric circulation.

04

Could its moons host oceans?

Some Uranian moons are candidates for further investigation of internal heating and the possibility of subsurface oceans.
