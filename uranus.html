Inside the ice giant

A very different kind of planet

Uranus is neither a rocky terrestrial planet nor a gas giant like Jupiter. Its deep interior contains different mixtures of volatile materials.

01 / ATMOSPHERE

Hydrogen, helium and methane

Uranus's atmosphere is composed mainly of hydrogen and helium, with methane contributing to its blue-green colour by absorbing red wavelengths of sunlight. Beneath the visible clouds, pressure and temperature increase dramatically.

02 / EXTREME TILT

A planet that rotates on its side

Uranus has an axial tilt of about 98 degrees. Its rotation axis lies nearly in the plane of its orbit, producing unusual seasons. During parts of its long year, one pole can face the Sun for years at a time. The cause of this extreme tilt remains an active scientific question.

03 / INTERIOR

Water, ammonia and methane-rich materials

Uranus is classified as an ice giant because its interior is thought to contain substantial amounts of water, ammonia and methane-related materials under extreme pressure. These are not necessarily ordinary surface ices; they exist under conditions very different from those on Earth.

04 / MAGNETIC FIELD

A strangely tilted magnetic field

Uranus's magnetic field is strongly tilted relative to its rotation axis and offset from the planet's centre. This unusual geometry creates a complicated magnetic environment that scientists still want to understand in greater detail.

05 / RINGS

Dark, narrow rings

Uranus has 13 known rings. Many are narrow, dark and difficult to see from Earth. Their particles range in size, and small moons and gravitational interactions help shape the ring system.

06 / TEMPERATURE

A cold and distant world

Uranus receives only a small fraction of Earth's sunlight. Its upper atmosphere can reach extremely low temperatures, and measurements suggest the planet emits relatively little excess internal heat compared with Neptune. Why this is so remains unresolved.

Why does Uranus look blue-green?

Methane in its atmosphere absorbs much of the red light entering the atmosphere. The remaining reflected sunlight gives Uranus its characteristic cyan or blue-green appearance.

The Uranian system

Explore Uranus's moons

Uranus has 28 recognised moons as of this page's reference point. Many are named after characters from works by Shakespeare and Alexander Pope.

Titania

Uranus's largest moon, with large canyons and impact craters that reveal a complex geological history.

Oberon

A distant, heavily cratered moon with a dark surface and signs of ancient geological activity.

Miranda

A small moon with striking cliffs, grooves and unusually varied terrain, making it one of the system's most intriguing bodies.

Ariel

A relatively bright icy moon with valleys and fault systems that point to a history of surface reshaping.

What might be hidden beneath the surface?

Some Uranian moons may have experienced internal heating in the past. Whether any still contain subsurface oceans is not yet known and needs further investigation.

Exploring a distant world

How we studied Uranus

Uranus remains one of the least explored planets, with most close-up information coming from a single spacecraft encounter.

1781

Discovery by William Herschel

William Herschel identified Uranus as a planet in 1781. It was the first planet discovered in the modern era using a telescope, expanding the known boundaries of the Solar System.

1986

Voyager 2

NASA's Voyager 2 made the only close flyby of Uranus to date in January 1986. It studied the atmosphere, rings, magnetic field and moons, discovering new moons and rings and returning valuable data about this distant planet.

Future

Proposed Uranus Orbiter and Probe

A dedicated orbiter and atmospheric probe has been recommended by planetary scientists as a high-priority future mission concept. It could study Uranus's interior, atmosphere, rings, magnetic field and moons in much greater detail. It is a proposed mission, not a spacecraft currently orbiting Uranus.
