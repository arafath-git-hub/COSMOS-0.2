JAMES WEBB Space Telescope

Look deeper into the universe with humanity's powerful infrared space observatory. The James Webb Space Telescope investigates the earliest galaxies, the birth of stars, distant exoplanets and the chemistry of worlds beyond our Solar System.

The Observatory

Why James Webb Matters

Webb extends our view beyond what visible-light observations alone can reveal.

Looking Back Through Cosmic History

Light takes time to travel across space. When Webb observes extremely distant galaxies, it detects light that began its journey billions of years ago.

The expansion of the universe stretches much of this ancient light into infrared wavelengths. Webb is designed to detect that faint infrared radiation and study early galaxies and cosmic evolution.

Seeing Through Cosmic Dust

Dense clouds of dust can hide regions where stars and planetary systems form. Infrared observations can reveal structures that visible-light telescopes may struggle to see.

Webb also examines exoplanet atmospheres, searching for signatures of molecules and learning about the conditions on worlds orbiting other stars.

Scientific Instruments

Four Tools for a New View of Space

Each instrument is designed for a different observing task. Together, they help Webb study distant objects in remarkable detail.

01 / NIRCAM

Near-Infrared Camera

NIRCam captures near-infrared images of galaxies, stars, nebulae and other celestial objects. It also helps scientists align Webb's primary mirror segments.

Imaging
02 / NIRSPEC

Near-Infrared Spectrograph

NIRSpec separates light into spectra so researchers can study the composition, temperature, motion and other properties of distant astronomical objects.

Spectroscopy
03 / MIRI

Mid-Infrared Instrument

MIRI observes longer infrared wavelengths, helping reveal cool objects, dusty environments, star-forming regions and distant galaxies.

Mid-Infrared Science
04 / FGS-NIRISS

Guidance Sensor and Near-Infrared Imager

The Fine Guidance Sensor helps Webb maintain precise pointing. NIRISS supports specialised imaging and spectroscopic studies, including investigations of exoplanets.

Guidance and Imaging
Engineering

Technology Built for Deep Space

Webb's design combines precision optics, thermal protection and a carefully planned deployment sequence.

The Golden Primary Mirror

Webb's 6.5-metre primary mirror is made of 18 hexagonal beryllium segments coated with gold. The segmented design allows a large mirror to fit inside the launch vehicle and unfold after launch.

The Five-Layer Sunshield

A tennis-court-sized, five-layer sunshield helps block heat from the Sun, Earth and Moon. Keeping the telescope cold is essential for detecting faint infrared signals from distant astronomical objects.

Operating Near L2

Webb operates around the Sun–Earth second Lagrange point, approximately 1.5 million kilometres from Earth. This location helps the observatory maintain a stable observing environment while keeping the Sun, Earth and Moon on the sunshield side.

A Carefully Unfolded Observatory

Webb launched in a folded configuration. Its mirror wings and sunshield were deployed in space after launch, a complex sequence that enabled the observatory to reach its operational form.

Mission Timeline

From Earth to the Infrared Universe

Major milestones in Webb's journey from launch to scientific operations.

25 DEC 2021

Launch

Webb launched aboard an Ariane 5 rocket from Europe's Spaceport in French Guiana.

JAN 2022

Arrival at L2

The observatory reached its operating region near the Sun–Earth second Lagrange point.

2022

Commissioning and First Images

Engineers aligned the mirrors, prepared the instruments and released Webb's first full-colour scientific images and data in July 2022.

Investigating the Universe

Webb observations support research into galaxies, star formation, exoplanets, planetary systems and the evolution of the universe.
