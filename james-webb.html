Infrared Space Observatory

James Webb Space Telescope

The James Webb Space Telescope, known as JWST, is a powerful space observatory designed to study the universe primarily in infrared light. It helps scientists investigate the earliest galaxies, the formation of stars and planetary systems, and the atmospheres of planets beyond our Solar System.

Space Agencies NASA, ESA, CSA
Launch Date December 25, 2021
Location Near Sun–Earth L2
Main Observing Range Infrared light

What Is JWST?

Webb is a space telescope with a segmented primary mirror about 6.5 metres across. Its large mirror collects faint light from distant cosmic objects. A five-layer sunshield protects the telescope from heat and helps keep its infrared instruments cold enough to operate.

Unlike a conventional visible-light telescope, Webb is optimized for infrared observations. Infrared light can reveal objects hidden by dust and can help scientists study light stretched by the expansion of the universe.

Mission Objectives

  • Observe some of the earliest galaxies that formed after the Big Bang.
  • Study how galaxies grow and change over cosmic time.
  • Investigate how stars and planetary systems form inside clouds of gas and dust.
  • Analyze the atmospheres of exoplanets to learn about their chemical composition.
  • Study objects within our Solar System, including planets, moons, comets, and asteroids.

How Does Webb Work?

Webb gathers infrared light using its primary mirror and directs it toward specialized scientific instruments. These instruments capture images and spectra that reveal details about the temperature, composition, and physical properties of astronomical objects.

Webb orbits the Sun near the second Sun–Earth Lagrange point, called L2, approximately 1.5 million kilometres from Earth. This location allows the telescope to remain aligned with Earth and its sunshield to help block heat and light from the Sun and Earth.

Major Scientific Contributions

  • Observed very distant galaxies whose light has traveled for billions of years.
  • Revealed detailed structures in star-forming regions and dusty cosmic environments.
  • Measured atmospheric signatures in selected exoplanets.
  • Studied the chemistry of planets, moons, and small Solar System bodies.
  • Provided new evidence to help researchers test theories of galaxy formation and planetary evolution.
Looking farther into space can mean looking further back in time, because light takes time to travel from distant objects to our telescopes.

Why Is Webb Important?

Webb allows scientists to investigate questions that could not be studied in the same detail with previous observatories. Its observations help researchers understand how the universe developed, how stars and planets form, and how planetary atmospheres differ from one another.

Although Webb can study exoplanet atmospheres, a possible chemical signature alone is not enough to confirm that life exists on another world.

Mission Status

Webb is an active scientific observatory. Its observations and discoveries continue to be released through scientific publications and official mission updates. Check NASA's website for current operations and the latest results.
