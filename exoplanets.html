COSMOS / EXOPLANET RESEARCH

Worlds Beyond Our Sun

Beyond our Solar System lies a vast population of distant worlds: scorching gas giants, rocky planets, ocean-world candidates and planets orbiting stars unlike our Sun. Exoplanet science investigates how these worlds form, what their atmospheres contain, and whether some might have conditions suitable for life.

02 / FOUNDATIONS

What exactly is an exoplanet?

Planets beyond our Sun are not all miniature versions of Earth. They differ in size, mass, temperature, chemistry, orbit and relationship with their host stars.

01 / DEFINITION

Planet beyond the Sun

An exoplanet is a planet orbiting a star other than the Sun, or in some cases a stellar remnant. Astronomers use multiple observations to distinguish planets from stars, brown dwarfs and other signals.

02 / DISTANCE

Light-years away

Many known exoplanets are tens, hundreds or thousands of light-years away. A light-year measures distance: the distance light travels in one year, about 9.46 trillion kilometres.

03 / EVIDENCE

Indirect discovery

A planet can reveal itself by dimming its star, pulling the star slightly, bending background starlight or reflecting and emitting light that telescopes can separate from the star.

04 / PLANETARY SYSTEMS

Not every solar system looks like ours

Some systems contain multiple planets packed close to their star. Others include giant planets on long, eccentric orbits. Their architectures preserve clues about the conditions in which planets formed and how their orbits changed.

  • Compact systems can fit several planets inside the orbit of Mercury.
  • Orbital resonances can link the periods of neighbouring planets.
  • Migration and gravitational interactions can rearrange planetary orbits.
  • Some systems contain planets orbiting two stars.
05 / HOW WE FIND THEM

Four major detection methods

Every technique has strengths and limitations. Combining methods often provides a more reliable picture of a planet's mass, radius, orbit and physical nature.

01

Transit photometry

When a planet crosses in front of its star from our viewpoint, it blocks a small fraction of starlight. A repeated dip in brightness can reveal the planet's orbital period and help estimate its radius.

Best for: Finding planets that pass in front of their stars.
Limitation: The orbit must be aligned suitably with our line of sight.
02

Radial velocity

A planet's gravity makes its star move around the system's shared centre of mass. Astronomers measure tiny Doppler shifts in stellar spectral lines as the star moves toward and away from us.

Best for: Estimating a planet's minimum mass and orbital properties.
Limitation: Stellar activity can imitate or obscure a planet signal.
03

Direct imaging

Special instruments and image processing suppress the glare of a host star so a planet's faint light can be detected. Infrared observations are especially useful for some young, hot giant planets.

Best for: Studying some planets far from their stars.
Limitation: The star is usually vastly brighter than the planet.
04

Gravitational microlensing

Gravity from a foreground star bends and magnifies the light of a background star. A planet around the foreground star can create a brief additional signal in the magnification pattern.

Best for: Discovering certain distant planets, including low-mass planets.
Limitation: The event is usually not repeatable, making follow-up difficult.
Additional methods include astrometry, which measures a star's tiny positional motion, and transit-timing variations, which look for changes in transit times caused by gravitational interactions.
06 / FROM SIGNAL TO DISCOVERY

How a planet becomes confirmed

A possible planet signal is only the beginning. Scientists test whether other astronomical effects could explain the observations.

Observe the target star

A telescope repeatedly measures the star's brightness, spectrum or position, producing a time series of observations.

Find a possible signal

Researchers search for repeating brightness dips, periodic Doppler shifts, unusual lensing patterns or a faint companion.

Rule out false positives

Binary stars, eclipsing systems, stellar activity, instrumental noise and data-processing effects can imitate planetary signals.

Collect follow-up observations

Other telescopes or instruments may measure the same target to test the candidate and improve estimates of its properties.

Estimate physical properties

Researchers combine the observations with models of the star and the orbit to estimate quantities such as radius, mass, density and orbital period.

Confirm and publish

When the evidence meets scientific standards, the object can be reported as a confirmed planet and added to recognised catalogues.

07 / READING THE LIGHT

What a transit can tell us

The shape, depth and timing of a transit contain information about a distant planetary system.

LIGHT CURVE

Brightness over time

A light curve records how a star's measured brightness changes. A shallow, repeated dip may indicate a planet crossing the stellar disk.

PLANET SIZE

Transit depth

For a simple transit, the fractional drop in light is approximately the square of the planet-to-star radius ratio. Stellar properties are needed for a physical radius estimate.

ORBIT

Repeating intervals

The interval between repeated transits gives the orbital period. With the star's mass and an appropriate orbital model, researchers can estimate the orbit's size.

08 / ATMOSPHERIC SCIENCE

Reading an alien atmosphere

Atmospheric studies help scientists investigate temperature, clouds, chemistry and how a planet interacts with radiation from its star.

Transmission spectroscopy

During a transit, a small amount of starlight passes through the planet's atmospheric edge. Different molecules absorb different wavelengths, leaving patterns in the observed spectrum.

Can investigate: water vapour, carbon dioxide, methane and other atmospheric constituents when the data and observing conditions allow.

Emission and eclipse spectroscopy

A planet emits thermal radiation and may reflect starlight. Comparing measurements before, during and after a secondary eclipse can help isolate its contribution to the system's light.

Can investigate: atmospheric temperature, heat redistribution, cloud properties and chemical signatures.

Detecting a molecule does not automatically establish that a planet is habitable or inhabited. Scientists assess the strength of the signal, possible alternative explanations, instrument effects and the wider planetary environment.
09 / HABITABILITY

What is the habitable zone?

The habitable zone is the range of distances from a star where a planet with suitable atmospheric conditions could potentially maintain liquid water on its surface.

CONDITION 01

Energy from the star

A planet's distance and the star's luminosity influence how much energy reaches the planet. The same distance can mean very different conditions around different stars.

CONDITION 02

Atmospheric greenhouse effect

Atmospheric gases, pressure, clouds and circulation can warm or cool a planet. Two planets at similar distances can have very different surface environments.

CONDITION 03

Planetary stability

Atmospheric retention, geological activity, stellar flares, magnetic environment and long-term climate evolution may affect whether liquid water can persist.

Being in the habitable zone does not prove a planet has oceans, a breathable atmosphere or life. It identifies a useful starting point for further investigation.

11 / EXTREME ENVIRONMENTS

Worlds that challenge our expectations

Exoplanet discoveries have revealed that planetary systems can produce environments far more varied than the familiar planets around our Sun.

EXTREME HEAT

Hot Jupiters

These giant planets orbit very close to their stars. Their intense irradiation can produce expanded atmospheres, strong temperature contrasts and complex atmospheric circulation.

LOW DENSITY

Inflated planets

Some gas giants have radii larger than simple expectations for their mass. Intense stellar heating and internal energy processes are among the factors scientists investigate.

ORBITAL EXTREMES

Eccentric worlds

Planets on elongated orbits may experience large changes in received stellar energy. Their climates and internal heating can differ greatly from planets on nearly circular orbits.

12 / SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE

Multi-planet systems and orbital resonance

When multiple planets orbit one star, their gravitational interactions can reveal how a system evolved.

Orbital resonance

Two planets are in a mean-motion resonance when their orbital periods are close to a ratio of small whole numbers, such as 2:1. Repeated gravitational interactions can become especially important.

Transit timing variations

If a transiting planet arrives slightly earlier or later than predicted, another planet's gravity may be responsible. These variations can help reveal additional planets and constrain their masses.

Planet migration

Planets may move from their formation locations through interactions with a protoplanetary disk or with other planets. Migration helps explain compact systems and some giant planets close to their stars.

13 / FORMATION

How do exoplanets form?

Planets grow from material surrounding young stars. Observations of young systems, disks and mature planetary systems help test models of this process.

A cloud collapses

Gravity causes part of a cold molecular cloud to collapse, forming a young star and a surrounding disk of gas and dust.

Dust grains collide

Small solid particles can stick together and grow into larger bodies, although collisions can also fragment or erode them.

Planetesimals and planetary embryos develop

Gravitational interactions help some solid bodies grow. The details depend on disk conditions and the available material.

Planets gather material

Growing cores can accrete solids and, under suitable conditions, capture large amounts of surrounding gas.

The system evolves

Disk dispersal, migration, collisions and gravitational scattering reshape planetary orbits and atmospheres over time.

14 / THE SEARCH FOR LIFE

Could an exoplanet support life?

Astrobiology investigates the conditions and processes that could allow life to originate and persist. Exoplanet observations contribute evidence, but no life beyond Earth has been confirmed.

Liquid water

Water is essential for all known life on Earth. Researchers investigate whether planetary conditions could permit liquid water, while recognising that life elsewhere might not follow every terrestrial pattern.

Atmospheric biosignatures

Certain gases may be interesting if they appear together in a context where biological processes are plausible. Each candidate must be evaluated against non-biological explanations.

False positives and false negatives

Geology, stellar radiation and atmospheric chemistry can produce gases that resemble potential biosignatures. Life may also exist without producing a detectable signal in a distant spectrum.

A possible biosignature is not a discovery of life. Strong conclusions require multiple lines of evidence, robust models and independent scientific scrutiny.
15 / STELLAR ENVIRONMENTS

The host star matters

A planet's environment depends not only on its own properties but also on the star it orbits.

Red dwarfs

Small, cool stars are common targets in exoplanet studies. Their habitable zones lie relatively close to the star, but stellar flares and high-energy radiation can complicate atmospheric evolution.

Sun-like stars

Studying planets around stars similar to the Sun helps scientists compare other planetary systems with our own. Accurate stellar age, radius and mass measurements are important for interpreting planets.

Stellar activity

Starspots, flares and magnetic activity can change brightness and spectral lines. Researchers must separate these effects from planetary signals, especially when looking for small planets.

16 / MEASUREMENT CHALLENGES

Why exoplanet research is difficult

Most exoplanets are tiny, faint and close in apparent position to a much brighter star. The observed signal must be separated from noise and other physical effects.

Stellar glare A star can outshine its planet by an enormous factor, making direct imaging technically demanding.
Instrument precision Small brightness and wavelength changes require careful calibration, stable instruments and repeated observations.
Model uncertainty Different combinations of atmosphere, clouds and surface properties may explain similar observations.

18 / LANDMARK DISCOVERIES

Discoveries that changed planetary science

Each milestone expanded the range of worlds known to exist and improved the tools scientists use to study them.

1992

Planets around a pulsar

Alex Wolszczan and Dale Frail reported planets orbiting the pulsar PSR B1257+12, demonstrating that planetary systems can exist around stellar remnants.

Historical milestone
1995

51 Pegasi b

Michel Mayor and Didier Queloz announced the discovery of a planet orbiting a Sun-like star. The hot Jupiter challenged earlier expectations about where giant planets could be found.

Landmark discovery
2009–2018

Kepler Space Telescope

Kepler measured stellar brightness with high precision and revealed that planets are common, including many smaller than Neptune.

Mission completed
2018–PRESENT

TESS

The Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite searches large areas of the sky for transiting planets, especially around relatively bright, nearby stars suitable for follow-up.

Mission active
2021–PRESENT

James Webb Space Telescope

JWST uses infrared observations to investigate the atmospheres of selected exoplanets and to study planets and disks in their formation environments.

Mission active
2019–PRESENT

CHEOPS

ESA's CHEOPS makes precise observations of known exoplanets, improving measurements of their sizes and helping constrain their bulk densities when mass data are available.

20 / SCIENCE FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Quick answers to common questions about distant planets and the search for life.

Have scientists found a second Earth?
Scientists have found rocky exoplanets and planets with some Earth-like properties. However, no known exoplanet has been shown to be a complete Earth twin with confirmed oceans, a suitable atmosphere and life.
Can we see an exoplanet with our own eyes?
No confirmed exoplanet is visible to the unaided human eye as a separate planet. A small number have been directly imaged using specialised astronomical instruments, while most are discovered through their effects on starlight or stellar motion.
How do scientists know a planet exists if they cannot photograph it?
Repeated and consistent signals can reveal a planet. Scientists test alternative explanations, collect follow-up data and compare independent methods before confirming a discovery.
What is the difference between a planet candidate and a confirmed planet?
A candidate is a signal that may be caused by a planet but needs additional analysis or validation. A confirmed planet has passed the relevant scientific tests for a planetary interpretation.
Can a planet outside the habitable zone have life?
The conventional habitable zone is based mainly on possible surface liquid water. Other environments, such as subsurface oceans heated by internal energy, are also considered in broader astrobiology research. Neither possibility establishes that life is present.
Can James Webb detect life on an exoplanet?
JWST can study selected exoplanet atmospheres and search for chemical signatures. Interpreting a possible biosignature is difficult, and no confirmed detection of extraterrestrial life has been made.
How long would it take to travel to an exoplanet?
Travel time depends on distance and spacecraft speed. Even the nearest known exoplanet systems are several light-years away, far beyond the reach of current conventional spacecraft for human travel on practical timescales.
Are all exoplanets orbiting normal stars?
No. Planets have been found around pulsars and other unusual stellar systems, and astronomers also investigate free-floating planetary-mass objects that are not currently bound to a star.
Why are super-Earths and mini-Neptunes important?
These size categories are common in exoplanet surveys but have no direct equivalent among the eight planets of our Solar System. Understanding their composition and atmospheric evolution helps explain how planetary systems develop.
Where can I find current exoplanet data?
The NASA Exoplanet Archive provides confirmed planet catalogues, candidate information, observational data and research tools. The European Space Agency and individual observatory teams also publish mission data and research results.
