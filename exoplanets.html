Research Field 03

Exoplanets

Discover worlds beyond our Solar System, from giant planets orbiting close to their stars to small rocky planets that may have conditions suitable for life.

What Are Exoplanets?

An exoplanet, or extrasolar planet, is a planet that orbits a star outside our Solar System. Some orbit stars similar to the Sun, while others orbit smaller, cooler stars or even multiple stars.

Thousands of exoplanets have been confirmed. They show that planetary systems can be very different from our own, giving astronomers new ways to understand how planets form, evolve, and interact with their stars.

Types of Exoplanets

Gas Giants

Massive planets dominated by hydrogen and helium, similar in broad composition to Jupiter and Saturn.

Super-Earths

Planets more massive than Earth but smaller than Neptune. Their composition can vary and is not determined by size alone.

Mini-Neptunes

Planets smaller than Neptune that may have thick atmospheres surrounding rocky or icy interiors.

Rocky Planets

Dense worlds that may have solid surfaces, although their temperatures and atmospheres can vary widely.

Hot Jupiters

Large gas giants that orbit extremely close to their stars, completing an orbit in a relatively short time.

Ocean Worlds

Possible water-rich worlds that may contain deep oceans beneath their surfaces or within thick outer layers.

How Do Astronomers Find Exoplanets?

Transit Method

A planet passes in front of its star and causes a tiny, repeating dip in the star's observed brightness.

Radial Velocity

Astronomers measure small shifts in a star's spectrum caused by the gravitational pull of an orbiting planet.

Direct Imaging

Special instruments block or reduce a star's glare to capture light from certain planets directly.

Gravitational Microlensing

A star's gravity bends and magnifies light from a more distant background star; a planet can add a detectable signal.

Astrometry

Scientists measure tiny movements in a star's position on the sky that can reveal the influence of an orbiting planet.

Atmosphere Studies

During suitable observations, starlight passing through a planet's atmosphere can reveal clues about its chemical composition.

The Habitable Zone

The habitable zone is the range of distances from a star where a planet with suitable atmospheric conditions could potentially maintain liquid water on its surface. Being inside this zone does not prove that a planet has water or life.

Scientists also consider atmospheric composition, planetary geology, magnetic activity, radiation, and the history of the planet's climate when evaluating its potential habitability.

Notable Exoplanet Missions

  • Kepler: Revealed that planets are common around stars in our galaxy.
  • TESS: Searches large areas of the sky for planets transiting nearby bright stars.
  • James Webb Space Telescope: Studies selected exoplanet atmospheres and other distant objects.

Questions Scientists Are Investigating

  • How common are Earth-sized rocky planets?
  • What are the atmospheres of distant planets made of?
  • Can scientists identify reliable signs of habitability?
  • How do planetary systems form and change over time?
  • Could any exoplanet support life as we know it?
