Engineering Challenges

Why building for space is different

Space systems must work in an environment where repairs are difficult, resources are limited and failure can end a mission.

Surviving the Space Environment

Engineers design systems to tolerate hazards that ordinary terrestrial equipment rarely encounters.

  • Vacuum: Materials and mechanisms must operate without normal atmospheric pressure.
  • Temperature extremes: Thermal control prevents components from overheating or freezing.
  • Radiation: Electronics and crews require appropriate shielding and fault protection.
  • Micrometeoroids: Spacecraft may need protective layers against high-speed impacts.

Operating Far from Earth

Distance changes how spacecraft are controlled, supplied and maintained.

  • Communication delay: Distant spacecraft cannot always respond immediately to Earth-based commands.
  • Limited power: Solar energy becomes less available farther from the Sun.
  • Reliability: Systems need testing, redundancy and fault recovery before launch.
  • Autonomy: Onboard systems help handle time-critical events when Earth is too far away.
Propulsion Lab

Different ways to move through space

No single propulsion technology is best for every mission. Engineers choose based on thrust, efficiency, journey time and mission requirements.

Technology
Main characteristic
Potential applications
Chemical rockets
Very high thrust
Launches and major manoeuvres
Electric propulsion
High propellant efficiency, usually low thrust
Satellite manoeuvres and deep-space missions
Solar sails
Uses photon momentum without conventional propellant for thrust
Specialized long-duration missions
Nuclear propulsion concepts
Potential use of nuclear energy for propulsion or onboard power
Future deep-space mission studies

Important: electric propulsion is efficient but generally cannot replace high-thrust chemical rockets for every phase of a mission. Nuclear propulsion concepts also differ widely in design and maturity.

Milestones

A timeline of space technology

Key developments that transformed spaceflight, satellite services and robotic exploration.

1957

Sputnik 1

The first artificial Earth satellite demonstrated that an object could be placed into orbit.

1961

First Human in Space

Yuri Gagarin's orbital flight marked a major milestone in crewed spacecraft engineering.

1969

Apollo 11 Moon Landing

Human lunar exploration demonstrated advanced launch, navigation, landing and life-support capabilities.

1977

Voyager Missions

Voyager 1 and Voyager 2 began journeys that expanded knowledge of the outer planets and interstellar space.

1981

Space Shuttle Era

The first Space Shuttle orbital mission introduced a reusable crewed spacecraft system, although its operations were complex and costly.

1998

International Space Station Assembly Begins

International partners began assembling a long-term orbital laboratory for science and human spaceflight research.

2000s

Reusable Rocket Development Advances

New launch-system approaches accelerated research into recovering and reusing rocket hardware.

2015

First Orbital-Class Booster Landing by SpaceX

Falcon 9's first-stage landing demonstrated a major step toward routinely recovering an orbital-class rocket booster.

2021

James Webb Space Telescope Launch

JWST demonstrated advanced deployable observatory engineering, including a large segmented mirror and sunshield.

2022

DART Asteroid Impact Test

NASA's DART mission changed the orbit of Dimorphos around Didymos, demonstrating a planetary-defence technique.

Engineering Notes

Facts worth knowing

Orbital Mechanics

A spacecraft in orbit is continuously falling toward Earth while moving sideways fast enough to keep missing the surface.

Vacuum

Space is not perfectly empty. It contains particles, radiation, magnetic fields and other forms of energy and matter.

Communication

Radio signals travel at the speed of light, but messages across interplanetary distances still experience significant delays.

Power

Solar panels can generate electricity in space, but available sunlight decreases with the square of distance from the Sun.

Reliability

Spacecraft are extensively tested because maintenance and replacement are often impossible after launch.

Robotics

Robotic missions can explore places where human presence would be difficult, dangerous or currently impractical.

Frequently asked questions

Quick explanations of common questions about space technology.

Space technology includes the spacecraft, launch vehicles, instruments, software, communication networks and other systems used to explore space and operate beyond Earth's atmosphere.
Rockets must accelerate their payload and much of their own mass. They also carry propellant that will be expelled during flight. The mass of propellant and the required change in velocity make launch a demanding engineering problem.
Spacecraft commonly use radio systems and antennas to transmit scientific data and receive commands. Large ground antennas and coordinated networks help maintain contact with distant missions.
Yes. A rocket produces thrust by expelling propellant. It does not need to push against air. This follows Newton's third law of motion and conservation of momentum.
A rocket is a propulsion system or launch vehicle that provides thrust. A satellite is an object placed in orbit around a celestial body. Rockets can carry satellites into orbit.
Ion engines accelerate charged particles and can use propellant very efficiently. Their thrust is generally low, so they are particularly useful for missions that can accelerate gradually over long periods.
Mars rovers use combinations of cameras, onboard computers, navigation software, wheel control and ground-team planning. Some systems can identify hazards and choose safer short routes autonomously.
Many spacecraft use solar arrays and rechargeable batteries. Missions operating far from the Sun or in environments with limited sunlight may use other power systems, including radioisotope power sources.
A reusable rocket has one or more components designed to return and fly again after inspection and refurbishment. Reuse can reduce some costs, but recovery, maintenance and operations still require resources.
Humans can live in space habitats for extended periods, but long-term settlement requires reliable life support, radiation protection, medical care, food production, maintenance and safe transportation. Many challenges remain.
In-situ resource utilization, or ISRU, means using resources available at a destination, such as lunar soil or water ice, to support exploration instead of transporting every material from Earth. Many applications remain under development or testing.
Physics, mathematics, computer science and engineering are useful foundations. Relevant specializations include aerospace, mechanical, electrical, materials, robotics and software engineering, as well as planetary science and astronomy.
