Research Field 05

Black Holes

Explore some of the most extreme objects in the Universe. Black holes reveal how gravity behaves under extraordinary conditions and help scientists investigate space, time, and galaxies.

What Is a Black Hole?

A black hole is a region of space where gravity is so strong that nothing, including light, can escape from within its event horizon. Black holes can form when massive stars collapse, and much larger black holes exist at the centers of many galaxies.

Black holes do not act like cosmic vacuum cleaners that pull in everything around them. From far away, their gravity behaves like that of any other object with the same mass. Objects must come close enough to be strongly affected.

Anatomy of a Black Hole

Event Horizon

The boundary beyond which escape is impossible. It is not a solid surface; crossing it means there is no known way to return outside.

Singularity

Classical general relativity predicts a singularity in a black hole's interior. The true physics there is not yet understood, and a quantum theory of gravity may be needed.

Accretion Disk

Gas and dust orbiting and heating around some black holes can glow brightly as material loses energy and moves inward.

Relativistic Jets

Some actively feeding black holes power narrow jets of particles moving at speeds close to the speed of light.

Photon Ring

Strong gravity can bend light into complicated paths around a black hole, contributing to bright ring-like structures in images.

Spacetime

A black hole's mass and rotation strongly curve spacetime, producing effects predicted by Einstein's theory of general relativity.

Important: The event horizon is the defining boundary of a black hole. The bright ring in a telescope image is light from hot material and strongly bent radiation, not a photograph of the event horizon itself.

Types of Black Holes

Stellar-Mass Black Holes

Form through the collapse of massive stars or related processes. They typically contain several times the mass of our Sun or more.

Intermediate-Mass Black Holes

These are between stellar-mass and supermassive black holes. Evidence for some candidates exists, but their population and formation pathways remain active research topics.

Supermassive Black Holes

These contain millions to billions of solar masses and are found at the centers of many large galaxies.

How Do Black Holes Form?

  • Stellar collapse: The core of a sufficiently massive star can collapse after its nuclear fuel is exhausted.
  • Mergers: Black holes can combine after orbiting one another and losing energy through gravitational waves.
  • Growth by accretion: A black hole can gain mass by pulling in surrounding gas and other material.
  • Early-Universe possibilities: Primordial black holes have been proposed as a possibility, but none has been confirmed.

How Do Scientists Detect Them?

Stellar Orbits

Stars orbiting an unseen, compact, massive object can reveal evidence for a black hole.

X-Ray Emissions

Extremely hot gas near a black hole can emit X-rays before crossing the event horizon.

Gravitational Waves

Detectors can measure spacetime ripples produced when massive objects such as black holes merge.

Event Horizon Telescope

A global network of radio telescopes combines observations to image emission around supermassive black holes.

Gravitational Lensing

A black hole's gravity can bend light from objects behind it, providing clues about its mass.

Accretion and Jets

Observations of energetic gas and jets can reveal how black holes interact with their surroundings.

Famous Black Hole Discoveries

  • Sagittarius A*: The supermassive black hole at the center of the Milky Way.
  • M87*: The supermassive black hole whose surroundings were imaged by the Event Horizon Telescope in 2019.
  • Gravitational-wave detections: LIGO and other observatories have detected signals from merging black holes.
  • Stellar motion studies: Observations of stars near the center of our galaxy provide strong evidence for a compact object with millions of solar masses.

Do Black Holes Last Forever?

According to theoretical physics, black holes may slowly lose energy through a process called Hawking radiation. For astrophysical black holes, this process would be extraordinarily slow, far longer than the current age of the Universe. Hawking radiation has not yet been directly detected from an astrophysical black hole.

Questions Scientists Are Investigating

  • How do supermassive black holes grow so large?
  • What happens to matter after it crosses the event horizon?
  • How can quantum physics and gravity be reconciled?
  • How do black holes influence the evolution of galaxies?
  • Can future observations reveal new types of black holes?
