Physics at the limit

How do black holes work?

Black holes are described by Einstein's general theory of relativity. Astronomers cannot directly see a black hole's interior, but they can investigate its influence on stars, hot gas, light, and gravitational waves. The event horizon itself is not a solid surface.

Gravity and orbital motion Track nearby stars and gas to estimate the mass of an unseen compact object.
X-rays and radio waves Observe hot material around black holes and the energetic environments near them.
Black hole shadows Use radio telescope networks to resolve the dark central shadow against emission from surrounding plasma.
Gravitational waves Detect space-time ripples from compact-object mergers and infer the properties of their sources.
Computer simulations Model relativistic gravity, magnetic fields, plasma, and light propagation near black holes.
A century of discovery

Major milestones

From mathematical predictions to direct observations, black hole science has transformed our understanding of gravity.

1915–1916

General relativity and the Schwarzschild solution

Einstein's theory describes gravity as the curvature of space-time. Karl Schwarzschild found a solution that includes the mathematical structure associated with a non-rotating black hole.

1960s

Quasars and compact objects

Discoveries of extremely luminous quasars strengthened the case that massive black holes can power energetic galactic nuclei.

1970s

Black hole thermodynamics

Research by Stephen Hawking, Jacob Bekenstein, and others connected black holes with entropy, temperature, and quantum theory.

1990s–2010s

Stellar orbits reveal Sagittarius A*

Decades of observations tracked stars orbiting an extremely compact, massive object at the center of the Milky Way, providing strong evidence for a supermassive black hole.

2015

First direct gravitational-wave detection

LIGO detected gravitational waves from a binary black hole merger, confirming a major prediction of general relativity and opening a new way to study black holes.

2019

The first black hole shadow image

The Event Horizon Telescope collaboration released an image of the shadow and surrounding emission of M87*, bringing a prediction of extreme gravitational lensing into direct view.

2022

The Milky Way's black hole is imaged

The Event Horizon Telescope released an image of Sagittarius A*, providing a new view of the black hole at our galaxy's center.

Today

A growing field of multi-messenger astronomy

Scientists combine electromagnetic observations, gravitational waves, and theoretical modelling to study black holes across a wide range of masses and environments.

Facts versus fiction

What is really true?

Popular descriptions of black holes can be misleading. Here is what modern physics says.

Scientific fact

Black holes do not vacuum up the entire universe

At a sufficient distance, a black hole's gravity behaves like that of any other object with the same mass. Nearby objects can orbit it safely if their trajectories allow it.

Scientific fact

The event horizon is not a solid shell

It is a boundary in space-time, not a material surface. An observer falling through a sufficiently large black hole's horizon might not notice a special local event at the crossing.

Important distinction

Black hole images are not ordinary photographs

The famous images show radio emission from hot plasma around a black hole and the dark central shadow created by strong gravity and light paths.

Theoretical prediction

Hawking radiation is not yet observed from an astrophysical black hole

The prediction is important in theoretical physics, but any such radiation from known astrophysical black holes would be extraordinarily difficult to detect.

Depends on mass

Not every black hole has the same tidal forces at its horizon

Tidal forces near the horizon of a stellar-mass black hole can be severe. For a supermassive black hole, those forces at the horizon can be much weaker.

Open question

We do not know the complete interior physics

Classical relativity predicts a singularity under idealized conditions, signalling that our current description may be incomplete where quantum gravity becomes important.

A black hole is a region of space-time bounded by an event horizon. Inside that boundary, no future-directed path allows light or matter to escape to distant observers.
A black hole itself emits no light from inside its event horizon. Scientists can observe its effects on surrounding matter and light. Event Horizon Telescope images show the dark shadow against bright emission from nearby hot plasma.
Tidal gravity may stretch the object, depending on the black hole's mass and the object's trajectory. Once it crosses the event horizon, it cannot send a signal back outside. The full interior physics is not yet understood.
There is no observational evidence that real astrophysical black holes provide traversable portals to other universes. Such ideas arise in speculative interpretations of some mathematical solutions.
