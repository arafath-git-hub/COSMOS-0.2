Research Field 08

Asteroids & Comets

Explore the small bodies of our Solar System: rocky asteroids, icy comets, meteoroids, and the fragments that sometimes reach Earth's surface. These objects preserve clues about our cosmic past.

Small Bodies of the Solar System

Not every object orbiting the Sun is a planet or dwarf planet. Asteroids, comets, and smaller fragments are remnants of the material from which the Solar System formed about 4.6 billion years ago.

Studying these objects helps scientists understand planetary formation, the delivery of water and organic compounds, and the impact hazards that can affect planets.

Types of Small Solar System Objects

Asteroids

Mostly rocky or metallic objects orbiting the Sun. Many are found in the main asteroid belt between Mars and Jupiter.

Comets

Icy bodies that can release gas and dust when warmed by the Sun, creating a glowing coma and sometimes long tails.

Meteoroids

Small pieces of rock or metal moving through space, often produced by asteroid collisions or comet activity.

Meteors

Streaks of light produced when material enters an atmosphere and heats the surrounding gas. They are commonly called shooting stars.

Meteorites

Fragments of natural space objects that survive passage through an atmosphere and reach the ground.

Dwarf Planets

Objects such as Ceres and Pluto that orbit the Sun and are rounded by their own gravity but have not cleared their orbital neighbourhoods.

Remember: A meteoroid is the object in space, a meteor is the light phenomenon in an atmosphere, and a meteorite is material that reaches the ground.

Where Are Asteroids Found?

Main Asteroid Belt

A region between Mars and Jupiter containing many rocky bodies orbiting the Sun.

Near-Earth Asteroids

Asteroids whose orbits bring them relatively close to Earth's orbital region. Their paths are monitored by astronomers.

Trojan Asteroids

Asteroids that share a planet's orbit while gathering around stable gravitational regions ahead of or behind the planet.

Why Do Comets Have Tails?

Many comets contain frozen water and other volatile substances mixed with dust and rocky material. As a comet approaches the Sun, its surface warms and releases gas and dust, forming a cloud called a coma.

Sunlight and the solar wind help produce two common tail components: a dust tail and an ion tail. Both generally point away from the Sun, though their shapes and directions can differ.

Famous Objects

Halley's Comet

A famous periodic comet that returns to the inner Solar System roughly every 76 years.

Asteroid Bennu

A near-Earth asteroid sampled by NASA's OSIRIS-REx mission. Its returned samples help scientists investigate early Solar System chemistry.

Comet 67P

The comet studied in detail by ESA's Rosetta mission, which included the Philae lander.

Ceres

The largest object in the main asteroid belt and a dwarf planet studied by NASA's Dawn spacecraft.

Chicxulub Impactor

The impactor associated with the mass extinction about 66 million years ago that ended the age of non-avian dinosaurs.

Interstellar Visitors

Objects such as 1I/'Oumuamua and 2I/Borisov passed through our Solar System after arriving from interstellar space.

Planetary Defense

Planetary defense is the work of finding, tracking, and characterising asteroids and comets that could pose an impact risk to Earth. Scientists calculate their orbits, improve observations, and study possible ways to reduce future hazards.

  • Detection: Search the sky for previously unknown objects.
  • Orbit tracking: Measure positions over time to refine orbital predictions.
  • Risk assessment: Estimate the probability and potential consequences of an impact.
  • Deflection research: Investigate whether a spacecraft could change an object's trajectory.

NASA's DART mission demonstrated that a spacecraft impact can change the orbit of a small asteroid moon. This was a technology test, not a response to an imminent threat to Earth.

How Do Scientists Study These Objects?

  • Ground-based telescopes: Track motion, brightness, and colour.
  • Radar observations: Help measure distances, rotation, shapes, and orbits for suitable nearby objects.
  • Spectroscopy: Provides clues about surface composition.
  • Spacecraft flybys and orbiters: Capture close-up observations.
  • Sample-return missions: Bring material to Earth for detailed laboratory analysis.

Questions Scientists Are Investigating

  • How did asteroids and comets contribute to the early Solar System?
  • What can their minerals and organic compounds tell us about Earth's history?
  • How many potentially hazardous objects remain undiscovered?
  • Can future missions reliably deflect a dangerous asteroid if needed?
  • What can interstellar objects reveal about other planetary systems?
