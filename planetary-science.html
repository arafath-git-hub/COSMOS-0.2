COSMOS / PLANETARY RESEARCH

Worlds Under Investigation

Planetary science explores how planets, moons, dwarf planets, asteroids and comets form, evolve and interact. By combining geology, physics, chemistry, atmospheric science and robotic exploration, researchers reconstruct the histories of worlds across our Solar System and investigate environments that may be suitable for life.

01 / THE BIG PICTURE

What is planetary science?

Planetary science is the interdisciplinary study of planets and other planetary bodies. It investigates their physical properties, composition, surface features, atmospheres, interiors, origins and potential habitability.

03 / PLANETARY GEOLOGY

Reading a world through its surface

Planetary geology uses landscapes, rock types, craters, faults and volcanic structures to reconstruct the history of planets and moons.

GEOLOGY 01

Impact craters

Asteroids and comets collide with planetary surfaces, excavating craters and exposing material from below. Crater size, shape and distribution help researchers estimate impact conditions and relative surface ages.

GEOLOGY 02

Volcanism

Volcanoes and lava flows reveal how heat escapes from planetary interiors. Earth and Venus show silicate volcanism, while Jupiter's moon Io is famous for extreme volcanic activity.

GEOLOGY 03

Tectonics and fractures

Faults, ridges, cracks and folded terrain can indicate stresses caused by internal cooling, tidal forces, crustal movement or changes in the volume of subsurface materials.

GEOLOGY 04

Erosion and sediment

Wind, flowing liquids, ice and gravity reshape landscapes. Dunes, channels and layered deposits help reveal environmental changes over geological time.

GEOLOGY 05

Mineralogy

Minerals preserve chemical and thermal information. Spectrometers and laboratory analyses can help identify hydrated minerals, volcanic rocks, salts and products of water-rock interactions.

GEOLOGY 06

Surface chronology

Scientists compare crater populations, stratigraphic relationships and radiometric ages of returned samples to build timelines of geological events.

04 / PLANETARY INTERIORS

What lies beneath a planet's surface?

Internal structure determines how worlds release heat, generate magnetic fields, reshape their crusts and evolve over billions of years.

Core

Dense material occupies the central region of many planets. Core size, composition and physical state influence planetary density and, in some cases, magnetic-field generation.

Mantle

The mantle transfers internal heat through processes that may include convection. Its mineralogy and temperature affect melting, volcanism and the movement of a planet's outer layers.

Crust and lithosphere

The outer rocky layers record impacts, tectonics, volcanic resurfacing and interactions with atmospheres. Their strength and thickness vary widely between worlds.

Interior models are often indirect. Scientists combine mass, radius, gravity, magnetic fields, seismic measurements and laboratory mineral physics to constrain structures that cannot be observed directly.
05 / ATMOSPHERES

Air, clouds and planetary weather

Atmospheric science examines gases, aerosols, clouds, winds, storms, energy transport and how atmospheres change over time.

Atmospheric composition

Spectrometers measure how gases absorb or emit light. Composition reveals chemical processes, atmospheric origins and interactions with a planet's surface.

Weather systems

Cloud bands, storms, jets and circulation patterns reflect atmospheric heating and rotation. Jupiter's Great Red Spot is a famous example of a long-lived giant-planet storm.

Greenhouse warming

Atmospheric gases absorb and emit infrared radiation. Differences in atmospheric composition and pressure help explain why Venus is extremely hot while Mars is cold today.

Atmospheric escape

Gas can escape through thermal motion, solar-wind interactions and other processes. Atmospheric loss can change a planet's climate and long-term surface conditions.

Clouds and hazes

Cloud particles and photochemical hazes affect how sunlight enters and infrared radiation leaves an atmosphere. They can obscure the surface and alter climate.

Comparative climates

Comparing Earth, Mars, Venus and giant planets helps researchers distinguish universal atmospheric physics from the effects of each world's unique environment.

06 / MAGNETIC FIELDS

Planetary magnetism and space weather

Magnetic fields reveal information about planetary interiors and influence how solar wind and charged particles interact with planets.

How a planetary dynamo works

In a conducting, moving fluid, electrical currents can generate a magnetic field under suitable conditions. Earth's liquid outer core helps maintain its global magnetic field.

  • Internal heat and fluid motion can power a dynamo.
  • Rotation influences the organisation of magnetic structures.
  • Different planets can have weak, strong, tilted or unusual magnetic fields.

Why magnetic fields matter

Magnetic fields shape magnetospheres, trap charged particles and create auroras. They also influence how stellar wind interacts with a planet's upper atmosphere.

  • Spacecraft magnetometers map fields and particle environments.
  • Magnetic data can help constrain subsurface conductive layers.
  • A magnetic field alone does not guarantee atmospheric retention or habitability.
07 / MOONS AND SATELLITES

Moons are worlds of their own

Natural satellites can possess atmospheres, volcanoes, ice shells, internal oceans and geological histories as complex as those of planets.

Earth's Moon

Lunar samples and orbital observations reveal the history of impacts, volcanism and the early Earth-Moon system. The Moon is also an important record of ancient Solar System conditions.

Io

Jupiter's moon Io is the most volcanically active world known. Strong tidal forces from Jupiter and interactions with neighbouring moons contribute to its internal heating.

Europa

Europa's fractured icy surface and magnetic evidence support the existence of a subsurface saltwater ocean. Its chemistry, ice shell and energy sources are important targets for habitability research.

Enceladus

Saturn's moon Enceladus ejects water-rich material from fractures near its south pole. Cassini measurements found salts and organic compounds in sampled plume material.

Titan

Titan has a thick nitrogen-rich atmosphere, methane weather and lakes of liquid hydrocarbons at its surface. Its chemistry provides a natural laboratory for studying complex organic processes.

Ganymede

Jupiter's largest moon has its own magnetic field. Multiple lines of evidence suggest it may contain a deep subsurface ocean beneath its icy crust.

08 / OCEAN WORLDS

Hidden oceans beneath ice

Some icy moons may contain liquid water beneath frozen surfaces, even where sunlight is weak and surface temperatures are extremely low.

Where does the energy come from?

Gravitational interactions between a moon and its planet, or between neighbouring moons, can flex a moon's interior and generate tidal heat. Radioactive decay may also contribute internal energy.

Ocean-world research examines whether water, chemical nutrients and usable energy could coexist in the same environment.

How can an ocean be detected?

  • Magnetic-field measurements can reveal an induced response from electrically conductive salty water.
  • Gravity and spacecraft tracking constrain internal mass distribution.
  • Surface fractures and geological features can reveal activity in an ice shell.
  • Radar can investigate the structure of ice and shallow subsurface layers.
A subsurface ocean is not proof of life. Scientists must also investigate chemistry, energy sources, environmental stability and whether potentially habitable conditions actually exist.
09 / PLANETARY HABITABILITY

What makes an environment potentially habitable?

Habitability research asks whether a world could provide conditions suitable for life as we understand it. It does not assume that life is present.

Liquid solvents

Liquid water is central to life as known on Earth. Scientists investigate temperature, pressure, chemistry and possible environments where liquid water could persist.

Chemical building blocks

Carbon-containing molecules, nitrogen, phosphorus, sulfur and other elements participate in terrestrial biology. Their presence alone does not demonstrate biological activity.

Usable energy

Sunlight, chemical reactions, tidal heating and internal heat can power planetary processes. Researchers ask whether an environment can sustain chemical disequilibrium and potential metabolism.

Planetary habitability depends on many interacting factors. A location inside a conventional habitable zone does not automatically have liquid water, a stable climate or life.
10 / FORMATION AND EVOLUTION

How the Solar System formed

Planetary formation models are tested against meteorites, young stars with disks, observed planetary systems and the chemical and dynamical properties of Solar System bodies.

A molecular cloud collapses

Gravity causes part of a cloud of gas and dust to collapse, forming the young Sun and a rotating protoplanetary disk.

Dust grains collide and grow

Solid particles accumulate into larger aggregates. Their growth depends on collision speeds, material properties and disk conditions.

Planetesimals and embryos develop

Gravitational interactions help some bodies grow into larger planetary building blocks.

Planets accrete material

Growing bodies gather solids, while some sufficiently massive cores capture substantial gas from the disk.

The system evolves

Migration, collisions, orbital resonances and gravitational scattering reshape the arrangement of planets and smaller bodies.

11 / SMALL BODIES

Asteroids, comets and dwarf planets

Small Solar System bodies preserve material and dynamical clues from the era when planets were assembling.

Asteroids

Many asteroids are rocky or metal-rich bodies, especially common in the region between Mars and Jupiter. Their compositions and orbits provide clues about the early Solar System and impact hazards.

Comets

Comets contain ice, dust and rocky material. As some approach the Sun, heating releases gas and dust, creating a coma and sometimes long tails shaped by sunlight and the solar wind.

Kuiper Belt objects

These distant bodies beyond Neptune include icy objects and dwarf planets such as Pluto. Their orbits and surfaces help researchers study the outer Solar System's formation and migration history.

12 / WATER AND PLANETARY CLIMATES

Following water through planetary history

Water can occur as ice, vapour, liquid, minerals bound to water or material trapped underground. Its distribution records temperature, pressure and geological history.

Ancient Mars

Martian valleys, deltas, sedimentary rocks and water-altered minerals provide evidence that liquid water once played an important role at the surface. The timing, duration and nature of those environments remain active research questions.

Earth and Venus

Earth retains abundant surface liquid water, while Venus has a dense, hot atmosphere and an extremely dry surface today. Comparing their histories helps scientists investigate how atmospheres, solar energy and geological processes influence climate evolution.

13 / IMPACT SCIENCE

Collisions that shape planetary worlds

Impacts influence planetary surfaces, contribute to the delivery of material and help reconstruct the early history of the Solar System.

Crater formation

The size and structure of an impact crater depend on impact speed, impact angle, gravity, target material and the energy released during collision.

Planetary defence

Tracking near-Earth objects helps assess impact risk. NASA's DART mission demonstrated that a kinetic impactor can change the orbit of a small asteroid moonlet around its companion.

Impact history

Comparing craters on different bodies helps scientists reconstruct collision rates and understand how resurfacing can erase evidence of older impacts.

14 / PLANETARY SCIENCE METHODS

How scientists investigate other worlds

No single instrument can answer every planetary question. Scientists combine complementary observations and models.

Visible-light imaging

Images reveal surface morphology, clouds, geological boundaries, craters and changes over time.

Infrared spectroscopy

Infrared measurements help identify minerals, gases, temperature patterns and thermal emission.

Radar mapping

Radar can map surfaces through some clouds and investigate roughness, topography and selected subsurface structures.

Gravity science

Tracking a spacecraft's motion helps reveal the gravity field and constrain the internal mass distribution of a planet or moon.

Magnetometry

Magnetometers measure magnetic fields and help investigate planetary dynamos, plasma environments and possible subsurface conductive layers.

Sample analysis

Laboratories can measure minerals, isotopes and organic compounds in returned material with techniques unavailable on many spacecraft.

15 / FROM DATA TO DISCOVERY

The scientific investigation process

Planetary discoveries are built through observation, testing, uncertainty analysis and comparison with independent evidence.

Define the scientific question

Researchers identify the problem: for example, whether a moon has an internal ocean or how a crater formed.

Select the right observations

Imaging, spectroscopy, radar, gravity, magnetic-field measurements or laboratory work are chosen to match the question.

Calibrate the instruments

Engineers and scientists account for instrument response, noise, geometry, temperature and spacecraft conditions.

Compare data with models

Researchers test whether proposed physical processes can explain the observations and whether alternative explanations remain plausible.

Publish and verify

Results are reviewed, shared with the research community and compared with future or independent measurements.

19 / LIMITATIONS AND OPEN QUESTIONS

What scientists still do not know

Planetary science often works with incomplete observations. A strong explanation must account for uncertainty and remain open to revision.

Hidden interiors

Most planetary cores and deep mantles cannot be directly sampled. Researchers use gravity, magnetic fields, seismic waves and laboratory experiments to constrain possible structures.

Ancient environments

Geological records are incomplete. Erosion, impacts and volcanic resurfacing can erase evidence, leaving several possible explanations for the same landscape.

Life beyond Earth

Habitability research identifies environments worth investigating, but no confirmed extraterrestrial life has been discovered. Future measurements must distinguish biological possibilities from non-biological processes.

Measurement uncertaintyEvery instrument has limits, calibration requirements and a finite ability to resolve small features.
Competing hypothesesScientists compare alternative models rather than assuming that the first plausible explanation is correct.
Independent evidenceConclusions become more robust when different instruments or methods support the same interpretation.
20 / PLANETARY SCIENCE FAQ

Frequently asked questions

Answers to common questions about planetary science, exploration and the search for habitable environments.

What is the difference between astronomy and planetary science?
Astronomy broadly studies celestial objects and the universe. Planetary science focuses on the origin, composition, structure, atmospheres, geology and evolution of planets, moons and smaller planetary bodies. The fields overlap extensively.
How do scientists study a planet without landing on it?
Orbiters and flyby spacecraft use cameras, spectrometers, radar, radio tracking and other instruments. Earth-based telescopes can also measure reflected light, thermal emission, atmospheric properties and orbital behaviour.
Why are there four rocky planets close to the Sun?
The inner Solar System was generally too hot for abundant water ice and many volatile materials to remain solid during planet formation. Rocky and metallic materials could accumulate there. The complete formation history also involved collisions, migration and material transport.
Why is Venus hotter than Mercury?
Venus has an extremely dense carbon-dioxide atmosphere that produces a powerful greenhouse effect. Mercury is closer to the Sun, but it has almost no substantial atmosphere to retain heat in the same way.
Is Mars still geologically active?
Mars is much less volcanically and tectonically active than Earth today, but it is not simply a completely unchanged world. Scientists investigate recent geological features, marsquakes, dust activity and evidence of past and possible present processes.
Could Europa or Enceladus support life?
Both are important targets because evidence suggests that they have subsurface oceans. Their potential habitability depends on chemistry, available energy, water-rock interactions and the persistence of suitable conditions. Life has not been confirmed on either moon.
What did the DART mission demonstrate?
NASA's Double Asteroid Redirection Test deliberately struck Dimorphos, a small moonlet orbiting the asteroid Didymos. The impact changed Dimorphos' orbital period, demonstrating the kinetic-impact technique under the tested conditions.
Why do planetary scientists study asteroids?
Asteroids preserve information about the material from which planets formed. Studying their composition, structure and orbits also supports planetary-defence research and helps identify potentially hazardous objects.
Can a planet have a magnetic field without Earth-like life?
Yes. A magnetic field is a physical phenomenon that can arise from processes in a planet's interior or from other magnetic interactions. It is not evidence that a planet contains life.
How do scientists determine a planet's age?
Scientists combine radiometric dating of suitable samples, crater counts, stratigraphy, geological relationships and models of planetary evolution. Different methods have different uncertainties and work best in different settings.
What is a planetary rover?
A rover is a robotic vehicle designed to move across another world's surface. It can carry cameras, spectrometers, environmental sensors and instruments that examine rocks and soil at close range.
Does finding water mean finding life?
No. Water can be present without life. Scientists also investigate chemical ingredients, energy sources, environmental stability and direct evidence before considering a biological explanation.
Where can I find real planetary science data?
NASA, ESA, JPL and research institutions publish mission data, instrument descriptions, scientific articles and educational resources. Links below lead to official agency websites.
