Saturn The Ringed World.

A magnificent gas giant surrounded by an immense system of icy rings. Saturn is home to extraordinary moons, including Titan with its methane lakes and Enceladus with its water-rich plumes. Together, these worlds offer some of the Solar System's most intriguing scientific mysteries.

What makes Saturn unique?

Its rings are spectacular, but Saturn's atmosphere and interior are equally fascinating.

01 / THE RINGS

A vast system of icy particles

Saturn's rings consist mainly of countless pieces of water ice, ranging from tiny grains to much larger chunks. The major rings extend hundreds of thousands of kilometres across, yet are surprisingly thin compared with their width. Gaps and waves within them are shaped by gravity, including interactions with Saturn's moons.

02 / ATMOSPHERE

Hydrogen, helium and powerful winds

Saturn's atmosphere is composed mostly of hydrogen and helium, with traces of other gases such as methane and ammonia. Pale cloud bands, fast winds and large storms circle the planet. Seasonal sunlight changes its appearance as Saturn travels around the Sun.

03 / INTERIOR

A deep, hot interior

Saturn has no ordinary solid surface. Pressure increases deep beneath the clouds, where hydrogen can become metallic. Its interior likely contains heavier materials concentrated toward the centre, but the exact structure and distribution remain subjects of scientific research.

04 / THE HEXAGON

A mysterious six-sided jet stream

A remarkably persistent six-sided pattern surrounds Saturn's north pole. This enormous atmospheric jet stream contains a central polar vortex. Scientists use it to investigate how rotating atmospheres produce stable geometric patterns.

05 / MAGNETIC FIELD

A surprisingly aligned magnetic field

Saturn has a large magnetic field generated inside the planet. Its magnetic axis is unusually well aligned with its rotation axis. This makes Saturn an important natural laboratory for understanding planetary magnetic fields and how they are generated.

06 / DENSITY

Less dense than water on average

Saturn's average density is lower than that of liquid water. The familiar claim that it could float in a giant ocean is a thought experiment: no real ocean could hold a planet of Saturn's size, and the planet is made largely of fluid and gas under extreme conditions.

Why do Saturn's rings shine?

Sunlight reflects from the rings' abundant ice particles. Their brightness and colour change with viewing angle, shadowing and the size of particles.

A family of worlds

Saturn's fascinating moons

Saturn has a large and changing catalogue of known moons. These six illustrate the remarkable diversity of its system.

Titan

Saturn's largest moon has a thick nitrogen-rich atmosphere, methane clouds, rivers and lakes of liquid hydrocarbons.

Enceladus

An icy moon that sprays water-rich plumes from fractures near its south pole. Evidence points to a global subsurface ocean.

Rhea

A heavily cratered icy moon with a bright, frozen surface and a long history recorded in its impact scars.

Iapetus

Known for its striking two-tone surface and an unusual ridge that runs along much of its equator.

Dione

An icy moon with bright fractures and evidence suggesting possible internal activity and a subsurface ocean.

Mimas

A small icy moon recognised by its enormous Herschel crater, which gives it a resemblance to a science-fiction spacecraft.

Two worlds of special interest

Titan's surface chemistry and Enceladus's ocean and water-rich plumes make both moons major targets for future astrobiology research. Neither has been confirmed to host life.

Robotic exploration

Our journey to Saturn

Spacecraft transformed Saturn from a distant point of light into a complex system of rings, storms and worlds.

1979

Pioneer 11

NASA's Pioneer 11 made the first close spacecraft flyby of Saturn, helping measure its environment and preparing the way for Voyager.

1980–81

Voyager 1 and Voyager 2

The Voyager spacecraft returned detailed images of Saturn's rings, atmosphere and moons. Voyager 1 made a particularly important close study of Titan.

1997–2017

Cassini–Huygens

The international Cassini mission orbited Saturn from 2004 to 2017. It studied the rings, atmosphere and moons. Its Huygens probe landed on Titan in 2005, delivering the first landing on a world in the outer Solar System.

Future

Dragonfly — NASA

Dragonfly is a planned rotorcraft mission to Titan. It is designed to investigate the moon's surface chemistry, prebiotic processes and habitability. Its planned launch and arrival schedule may change; consult NASA's official mission page for the latest timeline.
