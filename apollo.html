Apollo Program

The Apollo Program was NASA's historic effort to send humans to the Moon. It advanced spaceflight technology, expanded scientific knowledge about the Moon, and achieved the first human landing on another world.

Organization NASA
Program Era 1961–1972

What Was the Apollo Program?

Announced in 1961, Apollo was designed to develop the capabilities needed for human lunar exploration. The program included crewed test flights, lunar missions, spacecraft development, astronaut training, and scientific investigations.

Apollo used the Saturn V rocket to launch astronauts and spacecraft toward the Moon. The Apollo spacecraft included a Command Module for the crew's journey home and a Lunar Module for landing on and leaving the lunar surface.

Major Apollo Missions

  • Apollo 8 (1968): The first crewed mission to orbit the Moon.
  • Apollo 11 (1969): Neil Armstrong and Buzz Aldrin became the first two humans to walk on the Moon, while Michael Collins remained in lunar orbit.
  • Apollo 12 (1969): The second successful crewed lunar landing.
  • Apollo 13 (1970): An onboard oxygen tank failure forced the crew to abort the planned landing and return safely to Earth.
  • Apollo 15, 16 and 17 (1971–1972): Later missions expanded lunar surface exploration and scientific research. Apollo 17 was the final crewed Moon landing of the Apollo era.

What Did Scientists Learn?

  • Astronauts collected lunar rocks and soil for scientific analysis.
  • Experiments helped scientists investigate the Moon's structure, history, and environment.
  • Photographs and observations improved understanding of lunar geology.
  • Technology developed for Apollo helped advance guidance, computing, communications, and human spaceflight.
Why it matters: Apollo showed that humans could travel to another world, work on its surface, conduct scientific experiments, and return safely to Earth.

Is Apollo Still Active?

The Apollo lunar missions are complete. The spacecraft and equipment left on the Moon are historical artifacts, not an active crewed mission. Scientists continue to study the samples and data collected during Apollo, and later lunar programs build on the knowledge it produced.
