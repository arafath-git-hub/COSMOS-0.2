Research Field 07

Galaxies & Stars

Journey through vast collections of stars, glowing clouds of gas, and the life cycles of the objects that illuminate the Universe. Discover how stars form, evolve, and sometimes end in spectacular events.

What Is a Galaxy?

A galaxy is a gravitationally bound system containing stars, gas, dust, stellar remnants, and dark matter. Galaxies can contain millions to trillions of stars and vary greatly in size and shape.

Our Solar System belongs to the Milky Way, a barred spiral galaxy. The Sun is only one star among the enormous population of stars in our galaxy.

Types of Galaxies

Spiral Galaxies

These galaxies have a central region and spiral arms containing stars, gas, and dust. The Milky Way is a barred spiral galaxy.

Elliptical Galaxies

These range from nearly spherical to elongated shapes and often contain older stellar populations with relatively little cold gas.

Irregular Galaxies

These lack a clear spiral or elliptical structure and can be shaped by interactions, collisions, and ongoing star formation.

Lenticular Galaxies

These galaxies have a central bulge and disk but generally lack prominent spiral arms.

Dwarf Galaxies

Smaller galaxies that may orbit larger galaxies or exist on their own, with varied histories and stellar populations.

Galaxy Groups & Clusters

Galaxies are often found in groups and massive clusters connected by gravity and embedded within larger cosmic structures.

What Is a Star?

A star is a massive, luminous object made mostly of hot plasma. During most of its life, it releases energy through nuclear fusion in its core. Its mass strongly influences its temperature, brightness, lifetime, and final stage.

How Do Stars Form?

1. Molecular Cloud

Stars begin in cold, dense regions of gas and dust where gravity can cause material to collapse.

2. Protostar

As the collapsing material gathers, its centre heats up and develops into a young protostar.

3. Main Sequence

When stable hydrogen fusion begins in the core, the star enters the main-sequence stage of its life.

The Life Cycle of Stars

Stars do not all follow the same ending. Their later evolution depends strongly on their initial mass and on whether they interact with a companion star.

Low- and Intermediate-Mass Stars

After the main sequence, these stars expand into giants. Many eventually shed their outer layers, leaving a white dwarf.

Massive Stars

Massive stars can develop heavier elements in their interiors and may end their lives in a core-collapse supernova.

White Dwarfs

Dense stellar remnants that no longer sustain normal core fusion and gradually cool over time.

Neutron Stars

Extremely dense remnants formed from the collapsed cores of certain massive stars.

Supernovae

Powerful stellar explosions that can disperse newly formed elements into surrounding space.

Black Holes

Under suitable conditions, the collapsed core of a massive star can form a black hole.

Key idea: A star's mass is one of the main factors that determines its evolution. Not every massive star produces the same remnant, and not every supernova creates a black hole.

Nebulae: Stellar Nurseries and Remnants

A nebula is a cloud of gas and dust in space. Some nebulae are places where stars form, while others are material expelled by dying stars or debris left by stellar explosions.

Emission Nebulae

Gas glows after being energised by nearby hot stars or other energetic sources.

Reflection Nebulae

Dust scatters and reflects light from nearby stars rather than producing most of its own visible light.

Dark Nebulae

Dense clouds of dust block visible light from objects behind them and may contain star-forming regions.

Planetary Nebulae

Glowing shells of gas expelled by certain dying stars. Despite the name, they are unrelated to planets forming.

Supernova Remnants

Expanding debris and shocked gas left behind by a supernova explosion.

Star-Forming Regions

Dense areas inside molecular clouds where gravity can gather gas into new stars and planetary systems.

How Do Astronomers Study Galaxies and Stars?

  • Optical telescopes: Capture visible light from stars, nebulae, and galaxies.
  • Infrared astronomy: Reveals cooler objects and can observe through some dusty regions.
  • Spectroscopy: Helps identify chemical elements, temperatures, and motion.
  • Radio astronomy: Studies gas, magnetic activity, pulsars, and other radio-emitting sources.
  • Space telescopes: Observe wavelengths that Earth's atmosphere absorbs or distorts.
  • Stellar surveys: Measure the positions, distances, and properties of large numbers of stars.

Questions Scientists Are Investigating

  • How did the first stars and galaxies form?
  • How do galaxies grow through mergers and star formation?
  • How are elements created and distributed by stars?
  • What controls the formation and evolution of planetary systems?
  • How do central black holes influence their host galaxies?
