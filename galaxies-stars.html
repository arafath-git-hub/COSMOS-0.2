01 / Cosmic Foundations

Two scales of the universe.

Stars are the engines of many cosmic processes. Galaxies gather stars, gas, dust and dark matter into vast gravitational systems.

01 — STARS

Stellar engines

Stars form from collapsing clouds of gas and dust. Many spend most of their lives fusing hydrogen into helium in their cores.

02 — GALAXIES

Cosmic cities

Galaxies contain stars, stellar remnants, interstellar gas and dust. Their structures and histories are shaped by gravity and cosmic evolution.

03 — EVOLUTION

A changing universe

Stars create and distribute many chemical elements. Stellar explosions, gas flows and galaxy mergers help shape later generations of stars.

03 / Stellar Evolution

A star's life story.

A star's mass is one of the most important factors determining its evolution and final state.

Stage 01

Nebula

Gravity causes parts of a cold gas-and-dust cloud to collapse and form dense regions.

Stage 02

Protostar

A growing young object heats up as material falls inward. It may still be surrounded by a disk.

Stage 03

Main Sequence

Hydrogen fusion provides energy for a long-lived phase of stellar evolution.

Stage 04

Different Endings

Many stars leave white dwarfs; sufficiently massive stars can undergo core collapse, producing neutron stars or black holes.

This is a simplified overview. Stellar evolution differs significantly with initial mass, chemical composition, rotation and interactions with companion stars.

05 / Evidence Check

Cosmic facts to remember.

Astronomy combines observations, physical theory and uncertainty-aware estimates to understand objects far beyond our reach.

Stars have different colours.

A star's colour provides information about its surface temperature; blue stars are generally hotter than red stars.

Not every star ends in a supernova.

Many low- and intermediate-mass stars shed their outer layers and leave white dwarfs.

Galaxies contain more than stars.

They also contain gas, dust, stellar remnants and dark matter.

Looking far away means looking back in time.

Light takes time to travel, so distant galaxies are observed as they were when their light began its journey.

Most heavy elements have cosmic histories.

Many elements are formed through stellar processes, stellar explosions and compact-object mergers.

Visible galaxies are only part of the story.

Observations across radio, infrared, visible, ultraviolet, X-ray and gamma-ray wavelengths reveal different physical processes.

06 / Knowledge Check

Questions about stars and galaxies.

Answers to common questions about the objects that populate our universe.

A star is a luminous astronomical object held together by gravity. A galaxy is a much larger gravitational system containing stars, gas, dust and dark matter.
Stars form when dense regions within clouds of gas and dust collapse under gravity. The central object heats up, and sustained hydrogen fusion eventually begins if conditions are sufficient.
Estimates commonly place the number at roughly 100–400 billion. The exact number is uncertain because many stars are faint, distant or obscured by interstellar dust.
Initial mass is a major factor. Massive stars consume their nuclear fuel much faster and generally have shorter lives than lower-mass stars.
A supernova is an extremely energetic stellar explosion. Some result from the core collapse of massive stars; others involve white dwarfs in particular binary-star systems.
A neutron star is an extremely compact stellar remnant with a physical surface. A black hole has an event horizon, beyond which light cannot escape to distant observers.
Common broad categories include spiral, elliptical and irregular galaxies. Real galaxies can have complex structures and may not fit neatly into a single category.
Most stars belong to galaxies, but some stars can be found between galaxies after being displaced from their original systems.
For many nearby stars, astronomers use parallax: the apparent shift in a star's position as Earth moves around the Sun. Other methods extend distance measurements farther into the universe.
Gravity draws galaxies toward one another. Their stars rarely collide directly because distances between stars are enormous, although gas clouds and galaxy structures can interact strongly.
Spectral lines can reveal chemical composition, temperature, line-of-sight motion and clues about magnetic fields and other physical conditions.
Start with stellar evolution, gravity, light and the electromagnetic spectrum. Use public images and educational material from NASA and ESA, then learn how astronomers infer properties from observations.
