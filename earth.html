02 / Planet profile

The systems that make Earth work

Earth's surface and environment are shaped by processes that interact across land, water, air and the planet's interior.

Atmosphere

The air around our world

Earth's atmosphere contains mostly nitrogen and oxygen, along with smaller amounts of argon, carbon dioxide, water vapour and other gases. It helps regulate temperature, supports respiration for many organisms and shields the surface from much harmful radiation and many small meteoroids.

  • NitrogenAbout 78%
  • OxygenAbout 21%
  • Other gasesAbout 1% plus variable vapour
  • WeatherOccurs mainly in the troposphere
Oceans & water

A blue planet

About 71% of Earth's surface is covered by water. Most of that water is in the oceans, while freshwater is stored in glaciers, ice sheets, groundwater, lakes and rivers. Water moves through the water cycle, linking the atmosphere, land and oceans.

  • Surface covered by waterAbout 71%
  • Ocean waterAbout 97% of Earth's water
  • FreshwaterA small fraction of total water
  • Water cycleEvaporation, condensation, precipitation
Geology

A restless rocky planet

Earth's outer shell is divided into tectonic plates that move slowly over geological time. Their interactions help create mountains, ocean basins, volcanoes and earthquakes. Weathering and erosion continually reshape the surface.

  • CrustContinental and oceanic
  • MantleHot, mostly solid rock that can flow slowly
  • Outer coreLiquid metal
  • Inner coreSolid metal
Magnetic field

A shield shaped by Earth's interior

Motion of electrically conducting liquid metal in Earth's outer core generates its global magnetic field. The magnetosphere interacts with the solar wind and helps guide charged particles around our planet, though it does not block every form of radiation.

  • OriginMotion in the liquid outer core
  • Protective regionMagnetosphere
  • Visible effectsAuroras near polar regions
  • Field behaviourChanges over time
Why can Earth support life? Earth has persistent liquid water at its surface, a suitable range of temperatures for known life, essential chemical elements and energy sources. Life also changes the planet, especially its atmosphere and surface chemistry. Scientists continue to investigate how these conditions developed and how they may change.
03 / Inside Earth

Four major internal layers

Scientists study seismic waves, gravity, magnetic measurements and rock samples to understand Earth's interior, much of which cannot be observed directly.

Layer 01

Crust

The thin, solid outer layer. Continental crust is generally thicker and less dense than oceanic crust. Together with the uppermost mantle, it forms the rigid lithosphere.

Layer 02

Mantle

A thick layer of hot silicate rock. Although mostly solid, it can deform and flow slowly over long periods, helping drive plate tectonics.

Layer 03

Outer core

A liquid layer made mainly of iron and nickel. Its moving, electrically conducting material plays a central role in generating Earth's magnetic field.

Layer 04

Inner core

The innermost region is solid, mainly because immense pressure keeps its iron-rich material solid despite very high temperatures.

04 / Earth's companion

The Moon

Earth's natural satellite influences tides, preserves a record of ancient impacts and has been explored by robotic spacecraft and human astronauts.

Moon at a glance

  • Mean distance from EarthAbout 384,400 km
  • Mean diameterAbout 3,475 km
  • Orbital periodAbout 27.3 days
  • Natural satellites of EarthOne known Moon

Why the Moon matters

The Moon's gravity is the main driver of Earth's ocean tides, alongside the Sun's gravitational influence. The Moon's surface also preserves ancient craters and geological evidence that helps scientists reconstruct the early Solar System.

Earth and Moon are thought to have formed after a giant impact early in Earth's history, although researchers continue to refine the details of that event.

05 / Climate & life

A changing living world

Earth's climate system involves the atmosphere, oceans, land, ice and living organisms. These systems exchange energy and matter continuously.

The greenhouse effect

Greenhouse gases absorb and emit infrared radiation, keeping Earth's surface warmer than it would otherwise be. Human activities have increased atmospheric greenhouse-gas concentrations, causing global warming.

Biodiversity

Life on Earth ranges from microscopic organisms to enormous animals and complex ecosystems. Scientists have described millions of species, while many more may remain undocumented.

Earth observation

Satellites monitor clouds, sea level, vegetation, ice, fires and atmospheric composition. These measurements help researchers study long-term environmental changes across the entire planet.

06 / Space missions

Studying our own planet from space

Spacecraft help scientists observe Earth as one connected system. These are examples of important missions and observing programmes.

1972

Apollo 17

The crew's famous Earth photograph, often called the “Blue Marble,” became an iconic view of Earth from space. Apollo missions also returned valuable lunar samples.

Earth perspective
1999 onward

Landsat Programme

Landsat satellites have collected a long-running record of Earth's land surface, supporting research into forests, agriculture, water resources and urban growth.

Land observation
2000 onward

Terra

NASA's Terra satellite observes aspects of Earth's atmosphere, land and oceans, providing data used in climate and environmental research.

Earth system science
2014 onward

Orbiting Carbon Observatory-2

OCO-2 measures atmospheric carbon dioxide and helps researchers investigate where carbon is absorbed and released across the planet.

Carbon cycle
2014 onward

Global Precipitation Measurement

The GPM mission provides global observations of rain and snow, helping researchers study the water cycle and improve understanding of storms and extreme precipitation.

Water cycle
Continuous observations

Earth-observing satellites

Multiple international satellite missions measure temperature, clouds, ice, oceans, vegetation and atmospheric gases. Together, they provide a broad view of how our planet changes.

Global monitoring
07 / Open questions

What are scientists investigating?

Earth is our best-studied planet, but important questions remain about its deep interior, complex climate and the origins of life.

How did life begin?

Researchers study ancient rocks, chemistry and early environments to understand how non-living chemistry gave rise to the first living systems.

How will climate change?

Scientists combine observations and climate models to estimate future changes in temperature, sea level, rainfall and extreme weather as greenhouse-gas emissions evolve.

What lies deep inside Earth?

Seismic waves and other indirect measurements help scientists investigate the core, mantle dynamics and the processes that shape Earth's magnetic field.
