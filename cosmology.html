Cosmology

Explore the origin, structure, evolution, and possible future of the Universe. Cosmology investigates the biggest questions about space, time, matter, energy, and our place in the cosmos.

What Is Cosmology?

Cosmology is the scientific study of the Universe as a whole. Cosmologists use observations, physics, mathematics, and computer models to investigate how the Universe began, how it changes, and what it may become.

Evidence from distant galaxies, cosmic microwave background radiation, and the large-scale distribution of matter helps scientists test theories about the history of the Universe.

The Big Bang Theory

The Big Bang model describes how the Universe expanded from an extremely hot, dense early state about 13.8 billion years ago. It was not an explosion into empty space; rather, space itself has expanded over time.

The model is supported by several major observations, including the expansion of the Universe, the cosmic microwave background, and the abundance of light elements formed in the early Universe.

Expansion

On large scales, distant galaxies generally move apart as space expands, with the pattern measured through their redshifts.

Cosmic Microwave Background

This faint radiation is leftover light from the early Universe, released when it became cool enough for atoms to form.

Light Elements

The observed amounts of hydrogen, helium, and some other light nuclei broadly match predictions of early-Universe physics.

A Brief Cosmic Timeline

Early Universe

The Universe was extremely hot and dense, and it expanded and cooled as time passed.

First Atoms

After hundreds of thousands of years, conditions allowed electrons and nuclei to combine into neutral atoms.

First Stars and Galaxies

Gravity gathered gas into the first stars and early galaxies, gradually building cosmic structure.

Solar System Forms

Around 4.6 billion years ago, the Sun and Solar System formed from a cloud of gas and dust.

Today

The Universe is about 13.8 billion years old and continues to expand.

Dark Matter and Dark Energy

Dark Matter

Dark matter does not emit or absorb light in the way ordinary matter does. Its gravitational effects help explain galaxy motions and the formation of large-scale structures.

Dark Energy

Dark energy is the name given to whatever is driving the observed accelerated expansion of the Universe. Its fundamental nature remains unknown.

Ordinary Matter

Stars, planets, gas, dust, and living things are made from ordinary matter. It represents only a small fraction of the Universe's total energy budget.

How Do Scientists Study the Universe?

  • Telescopes: Observe galaxies, stars, and radiation across different wavelengths.
  • Spectroscopy: Studies light to reveal the composition, motion, and properties of distant objects.
  • Cosmic background measurements: Map ancient radiation left from the early Universe.
  • Computer simulations: Model how gravity and other physical processes shape cosmic structures.
  • Redshift measurements: Help scientists investigate cosmic expansion and the history of galaxies.

What Is Still Unknown?

  • What, if anything, happened before the earliest moments described by our current models?
  • What are dark matter and dark energy?
  • Why does the Universe contain more matter than antimatter?
  • Will cosmic expansion continue in the same way in the distant future?
  • How can scientists combine gravity with quantum physics?
